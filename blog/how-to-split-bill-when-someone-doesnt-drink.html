How to Split a Restaurant Bill When Someone Doesn't Drink

📅 6 August 2026 ⏱️ 5 min read ✍️ SplitEven Team
Quick Answer

Splitting evenly when some people don't drink isn't fair. Alcohol regularly accounts for 30–40% of a restaurant bill. The right approach: assign drinks to the people who ordered them, and split the food separately. A receipt scanning app makes this take about 30 seconds.

Someone declines a party invite because the host insists everyone splits the alcohol bill equally — even the non-drinkers. A woman says sharing a bill with a non-drinker made her not want to go out with that friend again. Another person quietly pays their £65 share on a £26 order because they don't want to make a scene.

These aren't edge cases. They're recurring situations across restaurants, dinner parties, and social events everywhere. And they're happening more frequently — Gallup's 2025 survey found that only 54% of Americans now drink alcohol, the lowest rate in nearly 90 years of tracking. That means in almost any group dinner, there's a real chance that someone doesn't drink, and the old assumption that "everyone had roughly the same" no longer holds.

The good news is that this is one of the most solvable bill-splitting problems. Here's how to handle it fairly, and how to do it without making anyone feel singled out.

Why the Even Split Is Unfair When Drinks Are Involved

The even split has one thing going for it: it's fast. But speed and fairness aren't the same thing, and the gap between them grows the more the group drank.

Here's a concrete example. Four friends go to dinner. The total bill is £140. Of that, £50 went on wine and cocktails — ordered by three of the four people. One person drank tap water all evening. An even split charges everyone £35. But the non-drinker's actual share of the food was around £22.50 — meaning they pay £12.50 toward drinks they never had.

That might not sound like much on one evening. But if this group goes out twice a month, the non-drinker has effectively paid for around £300 of other people's alcohol over a year. The people who drank have paid less than they should have by the same amount. Nobody chose this consciously — it just happened because the default was "split it evenly."

The Right Way to Split When Drinks Are Involved

There are two approaches, depending on how different the orders were.

Option 1: Split food evenly, split drinks by who ordered them

This is the cleanest method for most groups. Add up the food subtotal and divide it evenly among everyone — this works if food orders were roughly similar. Then assign each drink to the person who ordered it. Tax and tip apply proportionally based on each person's share of the total.

This method is easy to explain and easy for the group to accept. Nobody has to itemise their food order. The only thing that gets separated is the alcohol, which is clearly attributable to specific people.

Option 2: Split everything by what each person ordered

If food orders also varied significantly — some people had starters and dessert, others just had a main — the fully itemised split is fairer. Every item is assigned to whoever ordered it, and the app calculates each person's total from there. This takes slightly longer to set up but produces the most accurate result.

How to Do It Without the Awkwardness

The social dynamic is often harder than the maths. A few things that help:

Say something before you order, not after the bill arrives

At the start of the evening, it's completely natural to say: "Since not everyone's drinking, shall we just split food separately from drinks?" This frames it as a practical suggestion rather than a complaint. Most people will agree immediately — even the drinkers, who probably don't want their friends feeling put out.

Raising it after the bill arrives puts you in the position of pushing back against a suggestion that's already been made. It's the same outcome, but it reads differently in the room.

Use an app — and let the receipt do the talking

Scan the receipt with a bill splitting app. Assign drinks to whoever ordered them, assign food however the group decided. Share the breakdown with everyone.

When the itemised split comes from the receipt — not from you — it removes the interpersonal element entirely. Nobody is telling anyone they owe more. The receipt is. This makes it much easier for the group to accept the result without any awkwardness about who raised it.

Don't feel you need to justify not drinking

You shouldn't have to explain why you're not drinking in order to justify a fair bill split. The principle — that you pay for what you ordered — stands regardless of the reason. An app makes this easier by making the split feel routine and automatic, rather than a personal statement.

What About Other Mixed Situations?

Some people had starters, others didn't

Same principle: assign the starter to whoever ordered it. Their total goes up by the cost of the starter; everyone else's stays the same. The itemised method handles this automatically.

A round of drinks was bought but not everyone had one

If someone bought a round that included people who didn't want one, clarify at the time whether those people are included. If they were — fine, they can cover their share. If they weren't asked and don't want to be included, assign only the drinks people actually chose to have.

One person drank a lot, others had one drink

This works the same way — each drink goes on the tab of whoever ordered it. If someone had four cocktails and someone else had one glass of wine, that difference shows up in their individual totals. Frequently Asked Questions

Is it fair to split a restaurant bill equally when some people don't drink?
No — it's not fair. Drinks often account for 30–40% of a restaurant bill. When non-drinkers are asked to split the tab equally, they end up subsidising the drinks of everyone who did order alcohol. The fair approach is to separate food and drinks: split the food portion evenly (or by what each person ordered), and split the alcohol only among those who drank.

How do you split a restaurant bill between drinkers and non-drinkers?
The cleanest method is to use a bill splitting app with itemised receipt scanning. Scan the receipt, assign each drink to the person who ordered it, and assign food items to whoever ordered them. The app calculates each person's exact total including proportional tax and tip — so the non-drinker pays only for food, and drinkers pay for their own drinks.

What if friends insist on splitting the alcohol bill equally?
The easiest way to handle this is to let the numbers speak for themselves. Scan the receipt with a bill splitting app and share the itemised breakdown with the group. When everyone can see exactly who ordered what and what they owe, it's much harder to argue for an even split. The maths does the talking, so you don't have to.

How much do non-drinkers overpay when splitting a bill evenly with drinkers?
It depends on how much was drunk, but the numbers add up quickly. On a £120 dinner where £40 was alcohol split four ways, the non-drinker pays £10 for drinks they didn't have — and this happens every time the group goes out. Over a year of regular dinners, that's a meaningful amount of money.

What is the fairest way to split a restaurant bill when alcohol is involved?
Assign each drink to the person who ordered it. Use a receipt scanning app to do this automatically — scan the bill, tap each drink item and assign it to the right person, and the app divides the total fairly. Food can be split evenly among everyone, or itemised if orders varied significantly. Either way, non-drinkers pay only for food.
